DRAFT FACEBOOK POST — Tropical Storm Edouard:
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Tropical Storm Edouard made landfall near Port Arthur on September 1 with
sustained winds of 60 mph.[1] Tropical storm warnings covered Galveston Island,
Galveston County, Brazoria County, and Matagorda County, with a 1-to-2-foot
storm surge along the Galveston coast.[2] More than 75,000 Texas customers
lost power.[1]

If you hold a TWIA policy in one of those counties, the claims window is open now.

The first dispute most policyholders face after a tropical storm is not whether
the damage happened. It is which policy pays for which damage. Wind damage goes
to TWIA or your windstorm carrier. Flood damage goes to your federal flood policy,
if you have one. When a storm delivers both wind and water simultaneously, each
insurer points at the other. I've worked these disputes for years. That delay is
not an accident.

The second issue is documentation timing. Damage estimates written in the first
days after a storm capture visible damage only. Water that entered through
wind-driven openings migrates into wall cavities, roof decking, and insulation
over weeks. A claim closed early based on a first-pass inspection will miss
that damage.

TWIA's response timelines are set by Texas law. What adjusters do within those
timelines is their choice.

DRAFT FACEBOOK POST — San Antonio August 28 Storm:
----------------------------------------------------------------------
The August 28 windstorm in San Antonio produced measured gusts of 77 mph at
San Antonio International Airport and knocked out power for more than 111,000
CPS Energy customers.[1] San Antonio officials estimate the storm caused nearly
$10 million in damage.[2] I've seen what storms like this set in motion.

City inspectors surveyed 75 properties over the weekend, including 71 homes and
four commercial structures.[1] Two commercial buildings required emergency
demolition after structural assessments.[1] Two deaths are being investigated as
storm-related.[1]

When a storm produces this kind of documented structural damage, the insurance
process that follows is predictable. Adjusters arrive with wind speed data,
policy language, and a checklist. They look for ways to separate wind damage
from rain intrusion. They look for pre-existing conditions. They look for
depreciation arguments on roofing systems and siding.

Bexar County homeowners hold policies subject to Texas Department of Insurance
rules. Those rules require insurers to acknowledge claims, request documentation,
and issue payment or denial letters within fixed timeframes. What carriers choose
to do within those timeframes is a different matter.

A structural inspection report from a city engineer is a factual document. It
belongs in your claim file from the moment it exists. Adjusters know to use those
reports. Policyholders often do not.

The storm set the damage. The adjuster decides what gets paid.
