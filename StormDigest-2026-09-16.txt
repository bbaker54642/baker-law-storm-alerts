STORM: EF-0 tornadoes - Galveston County and Brazoria County, September 14, 2026
---
On Monday, September 14, the National Weather Service confirmed two tornadoes touched down in coastal Southeast Texas.[1] One hit Galveston Island, originating as a waterspout and moving onshore near the Holiday Inn on San Luis Pass Road. It blew out windows and damaged the exterior structure.[2] The second touched down in Brazoria County, about two miles east-northeast of Holiday Lakes. It destroyed a pump house and damaged a back porch.[2] Both were rated EF-0.

EF-0 is the lowest tornado rating. The wind speeds are approximately 65 to 85 miles per hour.[1] That is still enough to damage roofs, breach windows, tear off siding, and destroy outbuildings. The damage is real and it is covered under standard windstorm policies.

Galveston County and Brazoria County are in TWIA territory. A tornado touchdown in a TWIA-eligible county is a covered windstorm event. People who had property damage from the September 14 tornadoes should file a claim with their windstorm carrier.

Carriers will send adjusters, inspect, and make a coverage determination. EF-0 damage is real damage. Carriers sometimes write off smaller tornado events by citing pre-existing deterioration, minor damage thresholds, or deductible application. Those are choices the carrier makes. They are not limits on what the policy covers.

The NWS completed storm surveys and confirmed both tornadoes. That confirmation is on the record.

STORM: FEMA-4935-DR Amendment No. 9 - Aransas County, Nueces County, San Patricio County
---
On September 14, FEMA added Aransas County, Nueces County, and San Patricio County to a federal disaster declaration for the July 2026 severe storms.[1] Nueces County was also designated for Individual Assistance.[2]

I work in Aransas County. This declaration matters for people who had property damage from those storms and have not resolved their insurance claims.

Here is what the declaration does and does not do. It opens Public Assistance for local governments and eligible nonprofits.[1] In Nueces County, it also opens FEMA Individual Assistance grants for homeowners with uninsured or underinsured losses.[2]

It does not replace your insurance. FEMA Individual Assistance requires you to file your insurance claim first.[3] FEMA will not pay for losses your carrier covers, and FEMA grants are capped. The insurance claim is still the first and most important step.

Carriers know FEMA is in the picture. Some will sit on claims longer, knowing federal assistance may absorb losses they should pay. That is a choice they make.

If your insurance carrier has not responded fairly to a July storm claim in Aransas, Nueces, or San Patricio County, you still have time to dispute a low payment or a denial. The federal declaration confirms the damage was real.

STORM: Severe windstorm - Bexar County (San Antonio), August 28, 2026
---
People in San Antonio are right now sorting through insurance claims from the August 28 windstorm that knocked out power to more than 110,000 customers and left an estimated $10 million in damage across the city.[1] This week, federal records confirmed that an SBA disaster declaration was issued for Bexar County.[2] Qualifying homeowners and businesses may now apply for low-interest disaster loans to cover losses their insurance did not pay.[2]

A federal loan is not a substitute for what your policy owes you.

After straight-line wind events, carriers send adjusters quickly. The first estimate almost always comes in below the true cost of repairs. They call storm damage pre-existing deterioration or deferred maintenance. They apply depreciation that reduces your payment. If you dispute the figure, carriers invoke the appraisal clause, a provision that shifts the burden and the cost onto you to challenge their number.

The August 28 storm brought straight-line winds reaching 55 to 65 mph across San Antonio, with pockets of stronger wind confirmed by damage reports and radar.[1] That is wind damage. Standard homeowners policies cover it. Carriers choose to undervalue it, delay it, or call parts of it excluded. That is a choice they make. It is not a limit on what you are owed.

You paid your premium. You are entitled to the full cost to restore your property to its pre-storm condition, calculated at current material and labor prices.

STORM: Tropical Storm Edouard - Southeast Texas (Beaumont/Hardin/Polk), September 1, 2026
---
Communities in Beaumont, Hardin County, and Polk County took more than 20 inches of rain from Tropical Storm Edouard, with wind gusts near Port Arthur reaching approximately 90 mph.[1] Governor Abbott has requested a Presidential Major Disaster Declaration for the affected counties.[2] That request is pending.

What policyholders in these communities face now is the central question in every wind-and-flood storm: what caused the damage.

Standard homeowners policies cover wind damage, including wind-driven rain that enters through a damaged roof or wall. They do not cover rising water that enters from outside the structure. After Edouard, carriers arrived and began making that division. Wind damage goes to your homeowners carrier. Flood losses go to the National Flood Insurance Program, which carries its own payout caps and exclusions. When the cause is ambiguous, carriers assign it to the uncovered category.

Two additional mechanisms work against policyholders after a storm like this one. Depreciation disputes reduce the initial payment. Then carriers withhold recoverable depreciation until repairs are complete, which requires policyholders to fund work out of pocket before seeing their full settlement.

If a federal disaster declaration is approved, FEMA assistance can help cover what insurance does not pay. It does not replace what your policy owes. Your policy pays first.

The wind damage from Edouard is your carrier's obligation. The policy language does not change because the storm was large.
